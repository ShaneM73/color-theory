Step 1 · Light

Additive color: mixing light

Start with darkness. Each light source you switch on adds energy to the eye. Red, green and blue are the additive primaries because they line up with the three cone types in the human retina (long, medium and short wavelength). Red light plus green light reads as yellow, not because yellow photons appear, but because the L and M cones fire in the same ratio they would under real yellow light.

Mix all three at full strength and you get white. Mix none and you get black. The secondaries of additive mixing (cyan, magenta, yellow) are the primaries of the next station, which is not a coincidence.

Where it lives: screens, stage lighting, projectors, cameras, the sRGB and Display P3 color spaces.
multiply blend · C+M+Y = near black

Center overlap: #000000. Printers add a fourth ink, K (black), because real C+M+Y pigments only reach a muddy dark brown.

Step 2 · Pigment

Subtractive color: mixing paint and ink

Start with white light bouncing off white paper. Each pigment you lay down absorbs part of the spectrum and lets the rest reflect back. Cyan ink absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. Stack cyan and yellow and only green survives. Stack all three and almost nothing survives: black.

Painters learned this long before anyone measured a wavelength. The classroom RYB primaries (red, yellow, blue) are a historical approximation of the same idea; CMY is the version that gives a wider gamut and matches how the eye actually works.

Where it lives: printing (CMYK), paint, dyes, filters, watercolor. Also why mixing every paint on the palette gives you mud.

RYB (Itten, 1961)

Painter's wheel. Red, yellow, blue primaries; orange, green, purple secondaries. Complement of red is green.

RGB / HSV

CMY

Same hues as RGB, rotated 180°. Print's primaries sit where the screen's secondaries are.

Perceptual (OKLCH)

Hues spaced so equal steps look equal. Notice how little room pure yellow gets compared with the blues.

Step 3 · Maps

Four color wheels, four different truths

A color wheel is a map, and every map chooses what to distort. The painter's RYB wheel from Goethe and Itten puts the secondaries where pigments actually land. The RGB wheel is a mathematical ring of hue angles, perfectly even in numbers and uneven to the eye. The CMY wheel is the RGB wheel turned by 180°.

Modern perceptual spaces such as CIELAB, CIECAM and OKLCH rebuild the wheel around how far apart colors look. On that wheel yellow is a narrow sliver and blue takes up a wide arc, which is why designers who pick hues by RGB angle end up with palettes that feel lopsided.

Step 4 · Three dimensions

Hue, saturation, brightness

Mixing primaries tells you how colors are made; it says nothing about how to describe one. For that, every modern system uses three dimensions, first laid out by Albert Munsell in 1905 as hue, value and chroma.

Hue is the color's name on the wheel: red, orange, teal. In HSB it is an angle from 0° to 360°. Saturation (Munsell's chroma) is how much of that hue is present versus grey. At 0% every hue collapses to the same neutral. Brightness (value, or lightness) is how much light the color sends back. Drop it to 0% and every hue collapses to black.

The disc on the left is a slice of the HSB cone: hue runs around it, saturation from the centre outward, and the brightness slider dims the whole slice. Painters describe the same moves as tints (adding white), shades (adding black) and tones (adding grey).

Why it matters: a palette with varied brightness and one or two hues almost always beats one with many hues at the same brightness. Brightness carries hierarchy; hue carries mood. Squint at any good poster and the hues vanish while the design still reads.

Step 5 · Relationships

Color harmony

Harmony schemes are rules of thumb for which hues sit well together, expressed as angles on the wheel. Complementary pairs sit opposite each other and produce the strongest contrast; analogous hues sit side by side and read as calm; triads and tetrads balance the wheel geometrically.

Treat them as starting points. A working palette usually assigns roles (one dominant, one supporting, one accent) and varies lightness and saturation far more than hue. Most memorable palettes are nearly monochromatic with one deliberate complement.

Try it: set a triad, then drop saturation to 30%. The scheme survives; the garishness goes.

Sliders are HSL. Everything else is derived from the same sRGB value. The LAB numbers use the D65 white point.

Step 6 · Numbers

Color models: one color, many addresses

RGB is what the screen does. HSL/HSV rearranges the same cube into something humans can steer: pick a hue, then how vivid, then how light. CMYK is the printer's ink recipe. CIE XYZ is the measurement model everything is calibrated against.

CIELAB and OKLCH are perceptual: L is how bright it looks, and a numeric distance between two colors roughly matches how different they look. That is the property you want when generating palettes, checking contrast, or blending gradients without a muddy grey in the middle. CSS now supports oklch() directly.

Is HSB a cylinder? Mathematically, yes: hue is the angle, saturation the radius, brightness the height, and the three are independent, so the model fills a cylinder. But a cylinder is dishonest about what you see. At brightness 0 every point on the bottom disc is black, and at low brightness the saturation axis barely changes anything. So HSB is usually drawn as a cone that narrows to a black point, and HSL, where both ends collapse (to black at L = 0 and white at L = 1), as a double cone. The RGB cube needs no such trick: every corner is a distinct color, black and white sit at opposite corners, and the grey axis is the diagonal between them. Switch the solid above with the current color marked to see how the same color moves between shapes.

Gamut: sRGB covers about 35% of visible colors. Display P3 reaches roughly 45%. Printing reaches less than either, and in a different shape.

Step 7 · The observer

Perception: color happens in the brain

Wavelengths exist in the world; color does not. The retina reports three cone signals, and the brain turns them into an experience of color after comparing them with everything nearby, with its memory of what objects usually look like, and with a guess about the light source.

That last step is called color constancy. It is why a white page looks white under a yellow bulb and under blue sky, even though the light reaching your eye is completely different. The brain estimates the illuminant and discounts it. Most of the time this is a feature. Occasionally, when the scene gives it too few clues, it becomes a public argument.

Step 8 · Case study, February 2015

The Dress

On 26 February 2015 a Tumblr user posted a photo of a lace dress her friends could not agree on, asking whether it was white and gold or blue and black. Within hours it was the most-viewed post in BuzzFeed's history, split households (Kim Kardashian saw white and gold, Kanye West saw blue and black), and drew a verdict from the Prime Minister of Singapore. The photo had been taken by Cecilia Bleasdale, the bride's mother, on the Scottish island of Colonsay, and the dress was a £50 Roman Originals design.

The dress itself is royal blue with black lace. The photo, though, is badly exposed with a washed-out, bluish cast and no clear clue about the light source. Sample the actual pixels and you get a periwinkle (#7b8dc0) and a muddy brown (#5f4f32): neither white, gold, blue nor black. Those are the colors in the left frame above.

Your brain refuses to leave it at that. It applies color constancy, estimates an illuminant, and subtracts it. If it guesses the dress is standing in blue shadow or daylight, it removes blue, and the periwinkle snaps to white and the brown warms to gold. If it guesses the dress is lit by a warm indoor bulb and over-exposed, it removes yellow and darkens, and you get blue and black. Drag the slider to make either assumption on purpose.

Follow-up studies found the split tracks real differences between people: those who spend more waking hours in daylight lean white-and-gold, night owls lean blue-and-black. Most people cannot switch at will, and many never see the other version at all. Same photons, different priors, two sincere answers.

The lesson for designers: you never ship a color. You ship a stimulus, and every viewer finishes it in their own head, under their own lighting, on their own screen.
